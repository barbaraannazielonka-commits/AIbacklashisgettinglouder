What's happening
The issue is not hatred of technology, but the feeling of not being heard.

In May 2026, something unusual happened at university graduation ceremonies across the United States. When commencement speakers began talking enthusiastically about artificial intelligence, the graduating students booed.

At the University of Arizona, former Google CEO Eric Schmidt was interrupted multiple times with boos as he compared AI to the invention of the computer. At the University of Central Florida, real estate executive Gloria Caulfield was met with groans the moment she said, "The rise of artificial intelligence is the next industrial revolution."

The reaction wasn't random. These graduates have spent years being told not to use AI in their coursework. and are now entering the worst job market for young people in recent memory, where many job listings demand they "collaborate with AI." They feel the disconnect viscerally.

43% of young Americans (15–34) say it's a good time to find a job. down from 75% in 2022 (Gallup, 2026)
42% of Americans think AI will eliminate jobs in their field (CBS News Poll, 2025)
73% of AI experts think AI will positively impact work. vs. only 23% of the general public (Pew Research, 2025)
Lesson Plan Overview

When the Room Booed AI

A lesson in critical media literacy, technology ethics, and the politics of trust

⏱ 90 minutes 📚 Level B2 / C1 🎓 High School 📰 4 Real News Sources 💬 Group + Individual
1. Read and analyse authentic news articles about the AI backlash at graduation ceremonies
2. Identify and evaluate different perspectives on AI and the job market
3. Practise debating a complex, current issue using evidence from real sources
4. Write a structured opinion piece responding to one of the speakers' arguments
5. Discuss the difference between fear of technology and loss of trust in institutions
6. Reflect on your own relationship with AI in education and your futures
The Tasks
01
Vocab
Warm-Up · 15 minutes
Cracking the Code: Key Language Before You Read
⏱ Individual → Pairs

Before diving into the articles, make sure you have the language tools you need. Match each term below to its definition, then use it in a sentence of your own.

commencement speech
A formal address given to students graduating from a university. meant to inspire and celebrate their achievement.
backlash
A strong negative reaction from a group of people, often following a change or announcement they disagree with.
automation
Using machines or AI to perform tasks that humans used to do. often raising concerns about job losses.
entry-level job
A job for someone just starting their career, usually requiring little or no work experience.
tone-deaf
Failing to understand the feelings or concerns of others. saying something that seems ignorant of the mood in the room.
disruption
A major change that radically alters how an industry or system works. sometimes exciting, sometimes devastating.
skepticism
A questioning attitude. not necessarily hostile, but requiring evidence before accepting claims.
trust deficit
When a large group of people has lost confidence in an institution, leader, or system. a gap between what is promised and what is delivered.
  • Match each term above to its definition (They're already matched. Now write them in your own words without looking).
  • Choose three terms and write original sentences connected to your own experience with technology at school.
  • With a partner: which of these words best describes what happened at the graduation ceremonies? Defend your choice.
💡
Extension: The article quotes a graduate who says inviting an AI speaker was "a little bit tone-deaf." Without using the word "tone-deaf," how would you rephrase what she meant? Try three different ways.
02
Reading
Core Task · 20 minutes
Read Between the Boos: Analysing Real Headlines
⏱ Individual → Small Groups (3–4)

You have four news articles about the same events, written by different outlets. Read the key extracts and answer the questions that follow. Pay close attention to what each writer chooses to include. and what they leave out.

  • Read all four extracts carefully. Note the date, the outlet, and who is speaking in each one.
  • Perspective mapping: For each extract, identify (a) who is speaking, (b) what they want the reader to feel, and (c) one word or phrase that gives this away.
  • The numbers: Three statistics appear across the articles. 43%, 42%, and 73%. In your own words, explain what each statistic shows. Do any of them surprise you? Why or why not?
  • Spot the gap: Olivia Malone (University of Arizona graduate) says: "We as students are discouraged from using AI and penalised for using it. And then to have our speaker be the champion of AI is just like. OK? Why?" What contradiction is she pointing out? Do you think she has a valid point?
  • Headline challenge: Write your own headline for this story that is (a) neutral, (b) sympathetic to the graduates, (c) sympathetic to Eric Schmidt. Compare with your group.
🔍
Critical reading tip: Notice that Eric Schmidt himself acknowledges the students' fears during his speech. Does the fact that he heard them but kept going change how you feel about the situation? Discuss.
03
Debate
Speaking Task · 25 minutes
Whose Side Are You On? A Structured Debate
⏱ Teams of 3 vs. 3 · 2 min statements · 1 min rebuttals

Your class is divided into two sides. Use the evidence from the articles. and your own thinking. to make your case. You may be assigned a side you don't personally agree with. That's intentional: the best debaters can argue any position.

The Motion
"Graduates were right to boo AI speakers at their commencements."
✓ For the motion. Students were right

Arguments to develop:

  • Commencement speeches should inspire and respect the audience. not lecture them about technology that threatens their livelihoods
  • Students have been penalised for using AI in class. the hypocrisy is real and documented
  • Young people's unemployment rate (7.6%) is far higher than average. their fears are based on data, not just emotion
  • Booing is a form of democratic expression. silence would have been dishonest
  • Leaders who dismiss these fears without offering solutions have lost the right to be heard
✗ Against the motion. Graduates went too far

Arguments to develop:

  • Booing a speaker shuts down dialogue. exactly the kind of open debate needed to address AI concerns
  • Eric Schmidt specifically acknowledged students' fears and invited them to shape the future. they stopped listening
  • History shows technological revolutions (internet, computers) created more jobs than they destroyed over time
  • Rejecting AI expertise at graduation doesn't make the problem go away. it makes graduates less prepared
  • The booing targeted the messenger, not the message. and prevented nuanced conversation
  • Prepare (5 min): Each team selects their three strongest arguments and assigns a speaker for each. Find at least one specific quote or statistic from the articles to support each argument.
  • Opening statements (2 min each): Each side presents their case without interruption. No booing. ironic as that would be.
  • Rebuttals (1 min each): Respond directly to one argument made by the other side. Must reference something they actually said.
  • Class vote: After the debate, vote. not on who you agreed with going in, but who made the most convincing argument.
🎙
Language for debating at B2/C1: "While I understand your point about..." · "The evidence suggests, however, that..." · "This fails to account for the fact that..." · "Even if we accept that..., it doesn't follow that..."
04
Writing
Writing Task · 25 minutes (or homework)
Write the Speech They Should Have Given
⏱ Individual · 300–400 words · Opinion piece or speech

Eric Schmidt told graduates: "The future is not yet finished. It is now your turn to shape it." Many students felt this was too easy. a promise with no plan behind it. Your task is to write the speech they actually wanted to hear.

Your writing space
Write Your Headline Here
By [Your Name] · Class of 2026
  • Choose your format: A commencement speech (first person, spoken), a newspaper opinion piece (third person, formal), or an open letter to Eric Schmidt (second person, direct).
  • Your opening hook: Start with a fact, a question, or a short story. not a generic greeting. Remember: the graduates booed because they felt dismissed. Show that you hear them in your very first sentence.
  • Use the data: Reference at least two statistics from the articles. Paraphrase them. do not copy directly. Explain what they mean in human terms.
  • Acknowledge complexity: The best speeches don't pretend the problem is simple. Address one counterargument honestly. then explain why you still hold your position.
  • Your close: What is the one thing you want your audience to do differently after hearing you? End with that.
✍️
Evaluation criteria: Clear argument (does your position come through?) · Evidence use (do you back up claims?) · Register (is your tone appropriate for your format?) · Coherence (does each paragraph follow from the last?) · Engagement (would your audience keep listening?)
Discussion Questions

Let's Talk.

Nine questions across three levels of thinking

Comprehension → Analysis → Your Own Life
1
📖 Comprehension
In your own words, why did graduates boo commencement speakers who mentioned AI? Was their reaction about technology itself, or something else?
Refer to at least one specific quote from the articles.
2
📖 Comprehension
What specific contradiction did graduate Sami Wargo point out about AI and her education? Why does this contradiction matter?
Think about the gap between what schools say and what the job market expects.
3
🔍 Analysis
The CBS News article compares the current AI boom to the "dot-com era." What happened in the dot-com era. and why might that comparison worry young people today?
What does history tell us about technology hype?
4
🔍 Analysis
Eric Schmidt said the graduating class has "real power to shape how AI develops." Do you think this is a meaningful statement. or does it avoid the real question? What would "real power" actually look like?
Think about who currently makes decisions about AI development.
5
🔍 Analysis
The Pew Research Center found that as people learn more about AI, they become more skeptical of it. not less. Why do you think more knowledge leads to more doubt in this case? Is that always true with new technologies?
Consider other technologies people were skeptical of at first.
6
🔍 Analysis
There is a 50-point gap between AI experts (73%) and the general public (23%) on whether AI will positively impact work. What does this gap tell us? Who should we listen to. and why?
Think about the word "expertise". and its limits.
7
💬 Your Own Life
Are you allowed to use AI at your school? How do you feel about that policy. and does it match what you think the working world will expect of you in five years?
Be honest about your own experience.
8
💬 Your Own Life
If you were sitting in that graduation audience. would you have booed? Why or why not? Is there a difference between booing (which shuts someone down) and walking out (which is silent protest)?
Think about different forms of protest and what they communicate.
9
💬 Your Own Life
The article says the graduates didn't boo because they hate technology. they booed because they feel AI is "being done TO them" rather than built WITH them in mind. Where else in your life do you feel like decisions are made about you, without you?
This could be about school, society, government, or technology. How could that change?
